The Hario Switch and the Clever Dripper solve the same problem in the same way. Both let you steep ground coffee in a paper-filtered cone like a French press, then open a valve and drain a clean, sediment-free cup. Both cost less than $50. Both are easier to get right at 6 a.m. than any pour-over. And yet people who own one almost always have a strong opinion about why the other one is wrong.

Having dug through the live listings, the manufacturer pages, and a stack of side-by-side tests, the honest answer is that the "better" brewer depends on exactly two questions: how much coffee you make at once, and whether you ever want to pour like a pour-over. Everything below is organized around those two questions, plus the costs and annoyances that only show up after a month of ownership.

## Same idea, two different valves

Both brewers are "immersion drippers": a cone with a paper filter, a stopper at the bottom, and a release mechanism. What differs is the mechanism, and it shapes the whole experience.

The [Hario Switch Size 02](aff:B07NS2SV3W) is a heatproof glass V60 cone sitting in a silicone base. A stainless steel ball sits in the drain hole, and a plastic lever on the base moves it. Flick the switch up and the ball seals the cone so water pools. Flick it down and the cone drains straight through, exactly like a normal V60. Because the switch is independent of where the brewer is sitting, you can open and close it as many times as you like during a brew. That is the Switch's superpower: it is a pour-over dripper and an immersion brewer in one body.

The [Clever Coffee Dripper Large 18 oz](aff:B00EOM5RN0) is a one-piece BPA-free Tritan plastic cone with a rubber valve at the bottom that gravity holds shut. There is no lever. The valve only opens when you set the dripper on top of a mug or carafe, which pushes a pin up and releases the brew. It is foolproof in the literal sense: nothing happens until you move it onto a cup. It also means you cannot do a true pour-over with it, because every drop pools until the drain moment.

In one line: the Switch gives you a hand on the valve; the Clever takes the valve away from you entirely.

## Capacity is the real deciding factor

This is where most comparison articles go wrong by treating the two as the same size. They are not.

The glass Switch 02 holds 200 ml, roughly 7 fl oz of finished coffee. That is one modest mug. Hario also sells a [Hario Switch Size 03](aff:B09JL4R6SX) at 360 ml, about 12.5 fl oz, which brews a large mug or two small cups. The Clever Large is rated at 18 oz, with a practical fill of around 500 ml, and the smaller Clever is 300 ml.

Put prices against those capacities and the picture gets clear. Street prices at the time of writing: the glass Switch 02 lists at around $45 on Hario USA (Amazon hides the price behind a click), the Switch 03 around $49.50, and the Clever Large around $37 to $42 depending on which Amazon listing is in stock.

If you brew for two people, or you drink from a 16 oz travel mug, the Switch 02 is simply the wrong tool, no matter how nice the glass looks. If you only ever make one 7 oz cup at a time, the capacity gap stops mattering and the versatility argument takes over.

## The lid problem: heat loss during the steep

An immersion brew sits still for two to four minutes, and temperature matters during that steep. The Clever ships with a lid. The Switch does not have one.

One side-by-side test measured the Switch's brew water falling from 194°F to 164°F over a four-minute steep with the cone open to the air. That is a 30-degree drop, enough to pull extraction down and mute a light roast. The Clever's lid keeps far more of that heat in, which is a big part of why people describe Clever brews as fuller and the Switch as needing a hotter start or a shorter steep.

Workarounds exist: you can set a saucer over the Switch, preheat the glass aggressively, or keep steeps short and finish with a pour-over drawdown. But out of the box, the Clever holds temperature better and it is not close.

## Taste and versatility

Testers who have brewed both back to back tend to agree on the cup character. The Switch, especially when you use the switch mid-brew to combine a short immersion with a pour-over finish, leans toward a heavier body with brighter, sweeter notes. The Clever, with its longer still steep and lid, produces a cleaner, crisper cup with a smoother mouthfeel, closer to a very tidy French press.

The bigger difference is what else each one can do. The Switch is a complete V60 with a stopper attached. Open the valve and you get a normal fast-draining cone, which means you can use it for the same bright, expressive cups we covered when weighing [the cone-versus-flat-bed pour-over debate](v60-vs-kalita-wave-budget-pour-over-dripper.html). Close it and you have an immersion brewer. The popular "hybrid" recipes, where you bloom open, steep closed, then drain, are only possible on the Switch.

The Clever does one thing. It does that thing with less skill required than almost any brewer on the market, but if you ever want to learn pour-over technique, it cannot teach you.

## Filters: cost per cup and what the grocery store stocks

Both use paper, and both are cheap to run, but the paper is different.

The Switch takes standard Hario V60 02 filters (the 03 size takes 03 filters). A 100-count box of [Hario V60 02 white paper filters](aff:B001U7EOYA) was around $6.64 on Amazon at the time of writing, which works out to roughly 7 cents per brew.

The Clever Large takes ordinary Melitta-style #4 cone filters, the same ones a basic drip machine uses. A 100-count box of [Melitta #4 natural brown cone filters](aff:B00126IUZI) lists at $6.99 on Melitta's own store and was as low as about $4.50 at big-box retailers, so call it 5 to 7 cents per brew. The smaller 300 ml Clever uses #2 filters.

The cost difference is a rounding error over a year. The availability difference is not. Melitta #4 filters are in every supermarket in America; Hario 02 papers usually mean a specialty shop or an online order. If you have ever run out of filters on a Sunday morning, the Clever's grocery-shelf compatibility is a real quality-of-life point.

## Build quality and honest downsides

Neither brewer is perfect, and the flaws are different enough that they will decide the purchase for some people.

**Hario Switch downsides.** The body is glass, and a hard knock on a tile floor ends it. The switch lever is plastic and more than one long-term reviewer calls it the weak point of the design. There is no lid, so the heat loss above is baked in. It is heavier and less travel-friendly than the plastic Clever. And the 02 size is genuinely small for the money, which is why the price-per-ounce table looks the way it does. One more detail from the Hario listing: the plastic trigger sits in contact with the brewed coffee, which bothers people who bought glass specifically to avoid plastic contact.

**Clever Dripper downsides.** It is plastic, and it feels like it. Amazon reviews on the Large (4.7 stars across more than 4,000 ratings at the time of writing) are mostly glowing, but the recurring complaint is durability, with some owners reporting cracks over time. It is not dishwasher safe and not microwave safe, so it is a hand-wash item. The valve-on-cup design means you must never set it on a mug before the steep is done, or you will drain a 90-second brew by accident; everyone does this exactly once. And its lower extraction, the trade for that clean cup, can read as flat with very light roasts.

## A recipe that works in both

The nice thing about immersion is that one recipe carries across both brewers with only the release step changing.

1. Rinse the paper filter with hot water and discard the rinse. On the Switch, do this with the valve open so it drains; on the Clever, drain the rinse into your mug and dump it.
2. Close the Switch valve (or just leave the Clever sitting on its coaster). Add 15 g of medium-ground coffee for 240 ml of water, a 1:16 ratio. Scale up for the Clever Large: 30 g to 480 ml fills it comfortably. A cheap scale is worth having here; see [our favorite sub-$30 scales](best-budget-coffee-scales-under-30.html) if you are still eyeballing doses.
3. Pour all the water in at once, just off the boil for medium roasts, and give it a gentle stir to sink the grounds.
4. Steep 2 to 4 minutes. Longer steeps pull more body; shorter steeps keep things brighter. Put the lid on the Clever. On the Switch, cover it with a saucer if you want to fight the heat loss.
5. Release. Flip the Switch lever down, or set the Clever onto your mug. Drawdown takes about a minute on either.

If you want the Switch-only hybrid: bloom open with a little water for 30 seconds, close the valve, add the rest, steep 90 seconds, then open and let it drain. It is the best of both methods and it is the single strongest reason to pay extra for the Switch.

## Three immersion-brewer mistakes that ruin the cup

**Grinding too fine.** Immersion brews sit in contact with water far longer than a pour-over, so a fine grind over-extracts and clogs the drawdown. Both brewers want a medium grind, coarser than you would use for a V60. If your drawdown takes more than two minutes, go coarser. A consistent grind matters as much here as anywhere; the uneven output of a blade grinder is exactly the thing [a $45 burr grinder fixes](burr-vs-blade-coffee-grinder.html).

**Treating the Clever like a cup holder.** The Clever drains the instant it touches a mug. Steep on the coaster, then move. The number of people who have dumped a 60-second brew by resting it on their cup while reaching for the kettle is not small.

**Pouring the Switch from a stovetop kettle.** When the Switch valve is closed, your pour technique does not matter at all. When it is open, you are doing pour-over, and a sloppy stream from a saucepan undoes the advantage. If you plan to use the pour-over mode, [a controllable gooseneck kettle](best-budget-gooseneck-kettles-under-100.html) stops being optional.

## Which one should you buy?

- **Pick the Clever Dripper Large if** you brew for two, you fill a big travel mug, you want the lowest price per ounce, you want filters you can buy at the supermarket, or you want the brewer your least-coffee-interested family member can use without instructions. Its honest cost is a plastic body and no pour-over mode.
- **Pick the Hario Switch 02 if** you only ever make one 7 oz cup at a time, you already own or plan to learn V60 pour-over, and you want the hybrid recipes nobody else can run. Accept the glass, the missing lid, and the small size.
- **Pick the Hario Switch 03 if** you want the Switch's versatility but need a real mug's worth. At around $49.50 for 360 ml, it closes most of the capacity gap and is the version we would actually buy if the budget stretches.
- **Not sure immersion is for you at all?** You may already be served by [the AeroPress-versus-French-press choice](aeropress-vs-french-press-budget-brewer.html), which covers the same clean-cup-with-no-technique ground for less.

## Cheaper and second-hand alternatives

Both of these brewers are popular enough that the used market is deep, and immersion drippers have no electronics to fail. A couple of searches worth running before you pay full price:

- The discontinued [Bonavita Immersion Dripper](ebay:bonavita+immersion+dripper) was the original porcelain switch-style brewer, with a lever valve like the Hario's in a ceramic body that holds heat far better. It turns up used regularly and is a genuine bargain when it does.
- A used [Hario Switch ceramic](ebay:hario+switch+ceramic) solves the Switch's heat problem (ceramic retains warmth much better than thin glass) and lists new at about $50 on Hario USA, so a second-hand one can land near the price of the plastic Clever.
- If you want the clean immersion cup in a travel-proof body, a second-hand [AeroPress](ebay:aeropress) costs less than either brewer and is almost impossible to break.

Whichever way you go, both of these brewers reward exactly the same two upgrades as every other manual method: fresher beans and a better grinder. Buy the brewer that matches your cup size, then spend the savings on beans.

## FAQ

**Is the Hario Switch worth the extra money over the Clever Dripper?**
Only if you will use the pour-over mode or the hybrid open-close recipes. For pure steep-and-release brewing, the Clever does the same job for less, holds more, and keeps heat better thanks to its lid. If the switch mechanism is just a novelty to you, save the money.

**Which is better for beginners?**
The Clever. Its valve cannot be operated incorrectly, it comes with a lid and 100 filters, and the paper is stocked in every grocery store. The Switch is better for someone who already enjoys pour-over and wants one brewer that does both.

**Can the Clever Dripper do pour-over?**
No. The valve stays shut until the dripper sits on a cup, so water always pools. You can shorten the steep to a few seconds to mimic a faster brew, but it will never drain like a true cone.

**Do I need a gooseneck kettle for either brewer?**
Not for immersion. With the valve closed, you can dump water in from any kettle and stir. A gooseneck only matters on the Switch when you open the valve and use it as a V60.

**Which Hario Switch size should I get, 02 or 03?**
The 02 makes about 7 oz, which is one small mug. The 03 makes about 12.5 oz for roughly $4.50 more at Hario's list prices. Unless you brew strictly single small cups, the 03 is the better value.

**Does the plastic Clever affect the taste?**
Not that blind tasters can reliably detect. The Tritan body is BPA-free and the coffee contacts paper and plastic briefly. The bigger taste difference between the two comes from the lid and heat retention, not the material.
