Cold brew is the single most forgiving way to make genuinely great coffee at home, and it is almost insultingly cheap to get into. There is no pressure to hit, no water temperature to nail, no pour technique to practice for a month. You dump coarse grounds in cold water, wait, and strain. The result is a smooth, low-acid, naturally sweet concentrate that costs a café $5-6 a cup and costs you well under a dollar.

The catch — and there is always a catch on this site — is that the internet has quietly convinced people they need a $40 gadget to do something a mason jar has done for a century. You don't. This guide walks through the entire process end to end, tells you exactly what gear is worth buying (spoiler: very little), and spells out the four mistakes that turn cold brew from magic into muddy disappointment.

## The ratio that matters most

Cold brew is made as a **concentrate** and diluted to taste when you serve it. Get the ratio right and everything else is slack.

- **Concentrate ratio:** **1 part coffee to 4 parts water** by weight. That's roughly **1 cup of coarse grounds (about 100g) to 4 cups (about 470ml) of cold water.**
- **To serve:** dilute the concentrate **1:1** with water, milk, or over ice. Adjust from there — some like it 1:2 (weaker), some drink it neat over a big cube.

If you own a scale, weigh it; measuring by weight is far more consistent than by volume because grind fluffiness varies. Our [complete pour-over setup guide](complete-pour-over-coffee-setup-under-50.html) covers a $12 scale that's perfect for this and doubles for every other brew method.

## Grind size: the one thing people get wrong

**Cold brew needs a coarse grind** — think raw sugar or coarse sea salt, the same setting you'd use for French press. This is not optional. Long steep times over-extract fine grounds, and a too-fine grind is the #1 cause of bitter, muddy, silty cold brew that no amount of dilution fixes.

Pre-ground supermarket coffee is usually ground for drip (medium-fine) and will make cloudy, sediment-heavy cold brew. If cold brew becomes a habit, a burr grinder pays for itself fast — see our [best budget grinders under $60](best-budget-coffee-grinders-under-60.html); the manual picks there hit a clean, uniform coarse grind that ceramic-burr and blade grinders can't touch. If you already own a French press, you already have both a grinder setting and, honestly, a workable cold brew vessel.

## Step-by-step

1. **Grind coarse.** 100g of beans (about 1 cup of grounds) at a French-press-coarse setting. If you buy pre-ground, ask for "cold brew" or "French press" coarseness.
2. **Combine.** Add grounds to your jar or filter basket, then pour in 470ml (about 2 cups) of cold, filtered water. Stir gently to make sure every ground is wet — dry clumps float and won't extract.
3. **Steep.** Cap loosely and leave it **12 to 18 hours**. Room temperature is fine and slightly faster; the fridge is a touch cleaner-tasting and safer if you'll steep on the longer end. **Do not exceed ~24 hours** — past that you extract woody, bitter compounds.
4. **Strain.** Lift out the basket, or pour through the nut-milk bag into a clean jar. For extra clarity, run it once more through a paper coffee filter. Give grounds a gentle squeeze; don't wring aggressively or you'll push silt through.
5. **Store & serve.** Sealed concentrate keeps in the fridge for **up to 2 weeks**. To serve, dilute 1:1 with water or milk over ice.

## The four mistakes to avoid

- **Grinding too fine.** The big one. Muddy, bitter, silty results almost always trace back to grind size, not steep time.
- **Over-steeping.** More time is not more better. Past 18-24 hours you're extracting the bad stuff. Set a phone reminder.
- **Skipping dilution.** Concentrate is meant to be cut. Drinking it straight and then declaring cold brew "too strong" is a self-inflicted wound (though a small neat pour over one big ice cube is a legitimate style).
- **Using warm or old grounds and expecting drip flavor.** Cold brew tastes different by design — smoother, sweeter, lower in acidity and in the bright fruity notes hot water pulls out. That's the feature, not a bug.

## Cold brew vs iced coffee — a quick note

They are not the same drink. **Iced coffee** is hot-brewed coffee poured over ice, so it keeps the acidity and aromatics of a hot cup (and can taste watery as the ice melts). **Cold brew** is never heated, so it's smoother, sweeter, and noticeably lower in acid — easier on sensitive stomachs, but muted in the bright, tea-like notes some light roasts are prized for. If you love a fruity Ethiopian light roast, hot-then-iced may actually show it off better. For everyday drinking, dark and medium roasts shine in cold brew.

## FAQ

**How long does cold brew concentrate last?**
Sealed in the fridge, the undiluted concentrate stays good for up to two weeks, though it's best in the first 7-10 days. Once diluted with milk, treat it like any dairy drink and finish it within a couple of days.

**Can I use my French press to make cold brew?**
Yes, and it's a great starter move — steep the grounds in the carafe, then press slowly to strain. The only downside is that the French press's metal mesh is coarser than a paper or nut-milk-bag filter, so you'll get a bit more sediment. See our [AeroPress vs French press comparison](aeropress-vs-french-press-budget-brewer.html) if you're still deciding which immersion brewer to own.

**Is cold brew stronger (more caffeine) than regular coffee?**
The concentrate is much stronger, but you dilute it. A properly diluted serving lands in the same ballpark as regular coffee — though because the ratio is high, cold brew often ends up a bit more caffeinated cup for cup. Dilute more if you're sensitive.

**Do I need special "cold brew coffee" beans?**
No. Any whole bean works. Medium and dark roasts give the classic smooth, chocolatey cold brew most people love; light roasts work but their delicate acidity is partly what cold brewing tones down, so you may not get your money's worth from an expensive light roast here.

**Why is my cold brew cloudy and bitter?**
Almost always grind size. Too-fine grounds over-extract and pass silt through your filter. Grind coarser, and consider a second pass through a paper filter for clarity.

**Can I make a single cup instead of a big batch?**
You can scale the 1:4 ratio down (say 25g coffee to 120ml water in a small jar), but cold brew's whole advantage is making a batch once and drinking it all week. A small mason jar in the fridge is the low-effort sweet spot.
