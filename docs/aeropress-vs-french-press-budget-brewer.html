AeroPress vs French Press - Which Budget Brewer Should You Buy in 2026?

Published July 05, 2026 · Brewers

Ask the internet “what’s the best cheap way to make great coffee?” and two answers dominate every thread: the AeroPress and the French press. Both are immersion brewers (coffee steeps in the water rather than water passing through), both cost less than a takeout week of lattes, and both have devoted followings that talk past each other constantly.

They are not interchangeable. They make noticeably different coffee, they fit different mornings, and one of them has a cleanup ritual that quietly decides whether you’ll still be using it in six months. Here’s the honest head-to-head.

The 30-second verdict

  • Buy the AeroPress if you mostly brew for one, value easy cleanup, want the cleaner and more flexible cup, or travel with your coffee kit.
  • Buy the French press if you regularly brew for two or more at once, prefer a heavier full-bodied cup, or want the option of cold brew and the lowest possible price.

Taste: clarity vs. body

The AeroPress pushes brewed coffee through a paper micro-filter, catching both sediment and most of the oils. The result is a notably clean cup — closer to a good pour-over than to café press coffee — and short brew times keep bitterness in check even when your technique is sloppy. It’s also the more forgiving brewer with mediocre pre-ground coffee, which matters at this budget.

The French press filters with metal mesh only, so oils and micro-fines stay in the cup. That’s where its signature heavy mouthfeel comes from — genuinely rich and satisfying, especially with milk. The tradeoff is a silty last sip and a cup that punishes over-steeping with bitterness. (One free upgrade: after the plunge, pour all the coffee out immediately or decant it — coffee sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns harsh.)

Neither is “better.” Clarity vs. body is a preference, and it’s the main fork in this decision.

Capacity: the deciding factor most people ignore

The AeroPress brews one cup at a time, full stop. A second cup means a second two-minute cycle. Solo drinkers never notice; couples notice by day three. The French press brews for the whole table in one go, which is why it remains the default “guests are over” brewer even in houses full of fancier equipment.

If you brew for two-plus every single morning, this section outweighs everything else in the article: get the press, or budget for the $50 pour-over kit with a big server instead.

Cleanup: the six-month test

This is the sleeper criterion. The AeroPress ejects a dry, compact puck straight into the bin and rinses clean in ten seconds. The French press leaves a beaker of wet sludge that cannot go down most drains, wants a spatula-and-strainer routine, and has mesh that traps fines. It’s two minutes, but it’s two minutes every day — and it is the single most common reason presses migrate to the back of the cupboard.

Honest downsides on both sides: the AeroPress needs its tiny paper filters restocked (buy the 350-pack once a year) and its rubber plunger seal eventually wears (~2-3 years; replacement parts are cheap). The press’s glass beaker breaks — budget for one accident — or buy the stainless version and give up watching the brew.

Price and cost per cup

The press wins the sticker fight: Bodum Chambord 1L French press runs $25-35, and a perfectly good no-name 1L press is $20. The original AeroPress holds at ~$40 with almost no discounting, plus ~$4/year in filters.

Per cup, both are rounding errors next to café prices. Over five years the press’s only cost is a possible replacement beaker; the AeroPress adds maybe $20 total in filters and a seal. Call it a tie in practice.

Both brewers reward a decent grind more than any accessory purchase — a burr grinder from our under-$60 grinder guide upgrades either one more than any technique video. Note the press specifically wants a uniform coarse grind, which is exactly where the cheapest grinders struggle; AeroPress’s medium-fine target is easier on budget burrs.

The recipes that just work

AeroPress (standard, one cup): 15g medium-fine coffee, water just off the boil to the “3” mark (~220g), stir twice, steep 90 seconds, press gently for 30. Sour? Finer grind. Bitter? Shorter steep.

French press (1L, three mugs): 55g coarse coffee, 850g water off the boil, start timer. At 4:00, stir the crust, skim the foam, plunge slowly, decant everything immediately.

FAQ

Which one makes coffee closer to espresso? Neither makes espresso (that needs ~9 bars of pressure), but the AeroPress makes a small concentrated cup that takes milk like a flat white far better than press coffee does.

Is the AeroPress really worth $15 more than a press? If you brew for one: yes, on cleanup alone. If you brew for three: no — it’s the wrong tool regardless of price.

Can I do cold brew in these? The French press is genuinely excellent for cold brew (coarse grind, fridge overnight, plunge). The AeroPress makes a decent quick “flash” cold cup but isn’t a batch cold brewer.

Metal vs paper filter — is one healthier? Unfiltered coffee (metal mesh) carries cafestol, a compound that can nudge LDL cholesterol for heavy drinkers. Paper filters catch it. For a cup or two a day it’s a minor point, but it’s a real difference, not a myth.
