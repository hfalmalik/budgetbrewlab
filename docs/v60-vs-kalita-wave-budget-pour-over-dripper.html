V60 vs Kalita Wave: Best Budget Pour-Over Dripper 2026?

Published July 08, 2026 · Pour-Over

Ask ten pour-over drinkers which dripper to buy and you’ll start a small war. Half will tell you the Hario V60 is the only serious choice; the other half swear the Kalita Wave fixed everything the V60 gets wrong. Both are right, and both are missing the point — these two drippers are built around opposite philosophies, and the “better” one is simply the one that matches how carefully you want to pour.

Here’s the honest head-to-head, with the real tradeoffs and the ongoing filter costs nobody warns you about, so you buy the right $25 cone the first time instead of the wrong one twice.

The 30-second verdict

  • Buy the V60 if you like control, you own or plan to own a decent grinder and a gooseneck kettle, and you enjoy the ritual of dialing in a pour. It rewards technique with a cleaner, brighter, more expressive cup.
  • Buy the Kalita Wave if you want a great cup with less fuss, you pour a little sloppily, or you make coffee before you’re fully awake. Its flat bed and restricted flow forgive the mistakes that punish a V60.

If you never want to think about your pour again, skip to the pitfalls section — the single biggest difference between these two is forgiveness, and it decides everything.

One design difference explains the whole fight

Everything else follows from geometry.

The V60 is a 60-degree cone with a single large hole at the bottom and tall spiral ribs up the walls. Water runs down the cone, converges on that one big exit, and drains fast. Because the flow rate is largely up to you, the V60 hands you the steering wheel: pour speed, pour pattern, and grind size all change the cup dramatically. That’s the appeal and the trap.

The Kalita Wave is a flat-bottomed basket with three small holes in the base and a crimped “wave” paper filter that props the coffee bed away from the walls. The flat bed keeps the grounds level and evenly saturated, and the three little holes act as a flow restrictor — the water can only leave so fast, no matter how aggressively you pour. The Wave, in other words, takes the steering wheel back and drives more of the car itself.

Cone = control and upside. Flat bed = consistency and a safety net. That’s the entire debate in one line.

Taste: what you actually get in the cup

A well-executed V60 pour is the brighter, more transparent cup of the two. The fast, cone-shaped flow and thin coffee bed emphasize acidity, florals, and the high notes in a light-roast single origin — it’s the dripper competition baristas reach for when they want to show off a fancy Ethiopian. The catch is right there in the phrase “well-executed.” Pour carelessly and the same V60 gives you a thin, sour, unevenly extracted mess.

The Kalita Wave trades a little of that ceiling for a much higher floor. The flat bed and slower, regulated flow produce a rounder, sweeter, more balanced cup with more body. It won’t dazzle quite like a perfectly nailed V60, but it also almost never disappoints, and it flatters medium and darker roasts that can turn harsh and astringent in a cone. If your “great cup” and your “bad cup” need to be close together, the Wave is doing you a favor.

Neither is better in a vacuum. The V60 has the higher peak; the Wave has the higher average.

Filters, flow, and the cost nobody mentions

This is where a lot of buying guides go quiet, so here’s the part that actually hits your wallet over a year.

V60 filters are cheap, thin, and everywhere. A 100-pack of genuine Hario V60 02 filters runs about $7, generic equivalents even less, and every coffee shop and grocery stocks them. Call it $0.07 per brew.

Kalita Wave filters are pricier, fussier, and harder to find. The crimped wave shape can’t be substituted with a flat basket filter, genuine Kalita 185 filters cost around $10 per 100, and off-brand waves are hit-or-miss on quality. Call it $0.10 per brew — and factor in that you may have to order them online rather than grab a box on the way home.

That’s a ~43% higher filter cost per cup and a real convenience gap. Over a year of one-cup-a-day brewing it’s only about $11 in absolute terms, so it won’t break you — but the availability is the sharper edge. Run out of V60 filters and any store saves you; run out of Wave filters and you may be brewing something else until the mail arrives.

The pour-over pitfalls both drippers share

Whichever cone you pick, the dripper is the cheapest and least important part of a good pour-over. Two things matter more:

Grind consistency. Pour-over lives and dies on an even grind. A cheap blade grinder produces boulders and dust that channel and extract unevenly, and no dripper on earth fixes that. This is the single highest-leverage upgrade in the whole setup — spend here before you spend anywhere else, and see a burr grinder you can actually rely on for options that cost less than most people expect.

A controlled pour. Both drippers brew better when you can lay water down gently and precisely, and that’s a kettle problem, not a dripper problem. It matters more for the V60, whose whole personality is pour control, but even the forgiving Wave rewards a proper gooseneck kettle over a wobbly stream from a saucepan. The category-specific trap: people blame the dripper for a bad cup when the real culprit is grind or pour nine times out of ten. Buy either of these, fix those two variables, and you’ll be shocked how close the cups get.

The decision rule

  • You enjoy the process and want the most from great beans: V60. Accept that it demands a decent grinder, a gooseneck kettle, and a little practice — and that it’ll expose a lazy pour.
  • You want a reliably good cup with the fewest ways to screw it up: Kalita Wave. Pay the small filter premium and the occasional online reorder for a dripper that covers for you at 6 a.m.
  • You’re not sure you even like the pour-over ritual: start cheap. Grab the ~$8 plastic V60, and if the fuss isn’t for you, immersion brewers like the AeroPress get you a bold cup with zero pouring technique required.

The picks

For the cone camp, the Hario V60 02 clear plastic dripper at about $8 is the smartest entry point in all of coffee — it brews identically to the pretty versions and won’t crack. Want something that lasts a lifetime on the counter? The Hario V60 02 ceramic dripper (~$23) holds heat better and looks the part. Feed either with a 100-pack of Hario V60 02 paper filters (~$7).

For the flat-bed camp, the Kalita Wave 185 stainless steel dripper (~$28) is the durable everyday pick — steel won’t chip like the glass version and heats evenly. Smaller households can drop to the Kalita Wave 155 (~$25) for single cups. Stock up on genuine Kalita Wave 185 filters (~$10/100) so you’re never caught short, since the local grocery probably won’t carry them.

Both are outstanding drippers. The V60 is the enthusiast’s instrument; the Wave is the dependable daily driver. Pick the one that matches your patience at the counter, put the money you save into a better grinder, and you’ll out-brew most cafés either way.

FAQ

Is the Kalita Wave really more forgiving than the V60? Yes, and it’s the main reason to buy one. The flat coffee bed and three small flow-restricting holes even out uneven pouring and stop the fast, channel-prone draining that makes a careless V60 pour taste thin and sour. You can pour a Wave a little sloppily and still get a good cup.

Do I need a gooseneck kettle for either? Strongly recommended for the V60, whose whole character is pour control, and still helpful for the Wave. You can get by with a steady hand and a regular kettle on the Wave thanks to its flow restriction, but a gooseneck makes both drippers noticeably more consistent.

Can I use regular basket or cone filters instead of Kalita Wave filters? No — the Wave’s crimped, flat-bottomed filter is specific to the dripper and can’t be swapped for a standard flat basket or a V60 cone. That filter lock-in, plus the higher price and thinner availability, is the Wave’s main ongoing downside.

Which dripper is better for a beginner? The Kalita Wave, if your budget covers it — it hides technique mistakes while you learn. If money is tight, the ~$8 plastic V60 is a fantastic cheap way to find out whether you even like pour-over before spending more.

Does the dripper material (plastic vs ceramic vs steel) change the taste? Marginally. Ceramic and steel retain heat better, which helps in a cold kitchen, but a preheated plastic V60 brews so close to a ceramic one that most people can’t tell blind. Grind and pour matter far, far more than the shell.
